# **Using GPT-4 Turbo and Claude as Specialist Agents in a Digital Twin Healthcare System**

## **Capabilities for Specialist Clinical Tasks**

**Differential Diagnosis:** Both OpenAI’s GPT-4 (including the latest *GPT-4 Turbo* with health-centric tuning) and Anthropic’s Claude (e.g. *Claude 3* with extended medical context) have demonstrated strong diagnostic reasoning abilities. In formal evaluations, GPT-4 can generate highly accurate differential diagnosis lists, often matching or exceeding physician performance on clinical case vignettes  . For example, given text-based case descriptions, GPT-4’s top diagnosis was correct ~96% of the time on common cases (versus ~72% for physicians) . Even on challenging cases with uncertain presentations, GPT-4 included the correct diagnosis in its top 6 suggestions about 61% of the time, outperforming medical residents (44%) and attending physicians (49%) . These results underscore that GPT-4 can serve as a competent “digital specialist” for suggesting differential diagnoses. Anthropic’s Claude has likewise improved its medical reasoning: their latest *Claude* models (internally  *Opus 4.5* ) reportedly exceed 90% accuracy on a complex medical problem-solving benchmark (MedAgentBench), approaching expert-level performance . This suggests Claude is comparable to GPT-4 in generating plausible diagnoses. Both models can synthesize patient history, symptoms, and risk factors to propose likely conditions, making them suitable as diagnostic agents within a multi-agent system.

**Triage and Risk Stratification:** The models are also capable of triage reasoning – determining how urgent a case is and what next steps are needed. Studies comparing GPT-4 (as ChatGPT-4) to emergency physicians found it can capture detailed histories and suggest dispositions in an ER setting  . In one prospective trial, GPT-4 agreed moderately with clinicians on final diagnoses (κ≈0.54) and tended toward a **“safety-first” bias**, often recommending more tests or hospital admissions than human doctors . This indicates that as a triage agent, an LLM may err on the side of caution – flagging possible emergencies and advising further evaluation (a double-edged sword: safer but potentially over-utilizing resources). In a digital twin system, this conservatism can be tuned or moderated by other agents to balance safety with practical efficiency . Claude’s healthcare variant similarly is designed to help coordinate care and triage patient messages, sorting those that need urgent attention . Both GPT-4 and Claude can analyze symptoms or inbox messages and assign priority levels, making them useful as triage assistants (e.g. flagging “red-flag” symptoms for immediate follow-up). However, **reliability** in triage must be carefully evaluated – the models might miss subtle signs without proper prompting, or over-triage due to inability to fully gauge context like a clinician. Human oversight or calibrated rules are needed if these agents direct care urgency.

**Lab Result Interpretation:** Health-tuned LLMs excel at summarizing and explaining lab and test results in plain language. Claude’s healthcare model explicitly offers to *“explain test results in plain language”* for patients . For instance, a lab-specialist agent could take a set of lab values (blood tests, imaging findings, etc.) and output an interpretation: noting which values are abnormal and what they indicate. GPT-4 can do this as well, given its broad medical knowledge. It can compare a lab value against reference ranges and clinical context to identify concerns (e.g. flagging a high HbA1c as indicative of poor diabetes control). Both models can convert jargon or numeric ranges into an understandable summary for patients or a concise analysis for providers. In specialist roles (e.g. a “Lab Analyst” agent), the LLM could also generate a structured report: for example, outputting a JSON or FHIR **Observation** with interpretation. In fact, OpenAI’s GPT-4 Turbo introduced features for JSON-formatted outputs and function calling, which developers have used to reliably extract structured data like ICD-10 codes from text . Anthropic has added a dedicated *FHIR skill* to Claude that helps format data according to the FHIR healthcare standard for interoperability . Leveraging these capabilities, a lab interpretation agent could produce both a human-readable explanation and a machine-readable summary of results. A limitation to note is that LLMs rely on their training data for normal ranges and correlations – if a patient’s context (rare lab abnormalities, uncommon units) isn’t in training or provided in the prompt, the model might misinterpret it. Thus, high-stakes lab analyses may still require cross-checking against known reference databases or using tool augmentation (e.g. calling a calculator or medical reference).

**Treatment Planning and Recommendation:** Both GPT-4 and Claude can discuss treatment options and guidelines, but this is an area requiring **cautious use**. They have ingested vast medical literature and can output common treatment recommendations (for example, suggesting first-line therapies for hypertension or outlining pros/cons of a medication vs surgery). Indeed, GPT-4’s strong performance on medical exams implies it knows standard treatments for many conditions . However, giving actual treatment or diagnostic **recommendations** moves into the domain of clinical decision support that regulators classify as high-risk. The U.S. FDA’s draft guidance (Jan 2026) emphasizes that tools providing *“diagnostic or treatment recommendations”* likely qualify as medical devices requiring approval, as opposed to simpler administrative or educational tools . This means any direct treatment advice from an AI agent must be vetted and ideally kept *“for provider eyes only”* unless and until it’s validated. In a multi-agent setup, these models function well as **provider-level advisors** – e.g. a backend “consultant agent” that, given a patient’s case, suggests possible treatments or diagnostic tests to the clinician. They can synthesize clinical guidelines and patient specifics to propose a plan (Claude, for instance, can pull up relevant guidelines via its connectors to medical databases  ). As internal agents, they might output a ranked list of treatment options with rationale and even cite medical literature (especially if augmented with retrieval from sources like PubMed, which Claude’s platform supports ). The **limitation** is that without rigorous control, an LLM might hallucinate an unproven therapy or overlook patient-specific contraindications. Therefore, these specialist agents should act in an *augmenting* capacity – helping a doctor consider options – rather than fully autonomous prescribers. All treatment suggestions should be verified by a human provider or constrained by rules (e.g. only draw from trusted guidelines or formularies the system provides).

**Medical Q&A and Patient Education:** OpenAI and Anthropic models are widely used for general medical Q&A, both for patients and clinicians. OpenAI noted that by late 2025, hundreds of millions of health questions are posed to ChatGPT weekly . These models excel at answering questions about conditions, medications, wellness, and so on in a conversational manner. GPT-4 Turbo, for example, scores about **86%** accuracy on the MedQA benchmark (medical question-answering) , showing significant improvement in factual medical knowledge over earlier GPT-3.5 models. Claude’s knowledge base is similarly robust, and it has been adapted to explain health information at various reading levels. Within a digital twin system, a **Health Coach agent** can use these models to answer patient queries (“What does my MRI result mean?”, “How can I improve my sleep habits?”) or to provide ongoing education and motivational coaching. Both GPT-4 and Claude can adjust tone and complexity – for instance, the system could prompt the agent to answer at a 6th-grade reading level for accessibility . They can also clarify medical jargon: a patient-facing agent might translate “idiopathic neuropathy” to *“meaning the cause is unknown”* . One advantage of Claude is its very large context window (100k+ tokens in Claude 2, potentially more in Claude 3), which means it could ingest a patient’s entire longitudinal record or a lengthy healthcare plan and answer questions with that context in mind. GPT-4’s context window (up to 32k tokens in turbo versions) is smaller but still sufficient for most single-encounter data; it may require more selective summarization of records for context. Overall, for medical Q&A both models are quite capable, with differences in style: GPT-4 often provides succinct, structured answers, whereas Claude tends to be very detailed and cautious in explanations. Either can be programmed to include necessary **disclaimers** and friendly language when dealing with patients, as we will discuss in safety alignment.

## **Reliability and Limitations: Patient-Facing vs Provider-Facing**

While these models show  *impressive capabilities* , their reliability varies with how they are used and who the end-user is. It’s critical to distinguish **direct-to-patient interactions** from **provider-level decision support**, as the requirements and risks differ.

* **Direct-to-Patient Use:** Both OpenAI and Anthropic have been **very cautious** in positioning their models for patient-facing applications. The goal is to support patients in understanding their health, **not to replace professional medical advice** . For example, OpenAI’s newly introduced *ChatGPT Health* explicitly states it *“is not intended for diagnosis or treatment”* and is meant to *“support, not replace, medical care”* . It allows individuals to ask health questions, get explanations of their medical records, and receive wellness guidance – but always with the understanding that final decisions rest with healthcare professionals. The system includes prominent disclaimers and guardrails. Similarly, Anthropic’s Claude for Healthcare is designed to **acknowledge uncertainty** in its answers, include contextual disclaimers, and encourage users to consult clinicians for personalized guidance . In practice, this means a patient-facing agent built on GPT-4/Claude should **never present itself as a doctor** or give definitive medical decisions. Instead, it might say, “I’m an AI assistant, not a medical professional. Based on the information, it *could* be X or Y, but you should confirm with a doctor” . Both models are tuned to avoid hazardous instructions – for instance, they should refuse to directly prescribe medications or at least advise that a prescription requires a physician. The **reliability challenge** in patient-facing use is that a layperson might take the AI’s words as authoritative. Hence, the system must filter and frame the outputs carefully: providing helpful information and guidance (“Here’s what this term means… here are some questions you might ask your doctor”) without crossing into unsupervised medical advice. Another limitation is handling emotional or sensitive situations – patients might ask about dire diagnoses or personal fears. The AI needs to respond with empathy and encourage appropriate next steps (e.g. mental health resources if someone expresses suicidal ideation, or urgent care if symptoms sound dangerous) rather than just factual output. Both OpenAI and Anthropic have **safety layers** that attempt to detect and appropriately handle such cases (for example, refusing unsafe advice or providing crisis line info for self-harm queries). Nonetheless, continuous monitoring and human fallback channels are recommended when deploying these agents to the general public.
* **Provider-Facing Use:** When these models act as tools for clinicians or within a healthcare team (not directly visible to the patient), we can generally allow them to be more **forthright and technical**, but reliability is still paramount. In a provider-facing role, an LLM agent can generate differential diagnoses, summarize patient records, draft documentation, or suggest treatment options as a “second opinion.” The advantage is that a licensed professional is in the loop to vet the AI’s output. For example, a doctor receiving a differential from a GPT-4 agent can use it as a prompt for their own reasoning but will verify each suggestion. This reduces the risk of blind acceptance of a flawed AI answer. Studies indicate that clinicians *augmented* by GPT-4 in diagnostic tasks perform about as well as those unaided , suggesting the AI can be a helpful adjunct but not a magic bullet. Key reliability issues in provider-facing use include **hallucinations** (the model may output a condition or fact that is entirely false) and **out-of-date knowledge**. If GPT-4 or Claude’s knowledge cutoff is, say, 2024, a new drug or guideline from 2025 might be missed or answered incorrectly. To mitigate this, one can integrate Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), feeding the model up-to-date references (e.g. recent journal articles or hospital protocols) when asking clinical questions. Another limitation is that the AI might occasionally produce an answer that *sounds* authoritative but is subtly incorrect or not applicable to the specific patient – a busy provider might not catch the error. Therefore, many enterprise deployments require a form of **verification or double review**. For instance, if the AI drafts a clinical note or a response to a patient message, the clinician reviews and signs off on it. Anecdotally, providers find the suggestions useful for efficiency (OpenAI’s early hospital partners reported 30–40% reductions in documentation time using GPT-4 for note drafting, under human oversight ). Yet, they also note that the AI output often needs editing for accuracy and completeness. In summary, at the provider level these models are reliable enough to  *accelerate routine tasks and provide decision support* , but not so reliable that one should follow them unquestioningly. Clear **audit trails** and human validation steps are crucial here.

**Common Limitations & Mitigations:** Both GPT-4 and Claude share some general limitations. They lack true real-time sensory input or the ability to ask clarifying questions unless programmed to do so. If a patient’s description is incomplete, the model might assume or ignore missing pieces – whereas a doctor might dig deeper. One way to address this is via a conversational intake (the AI, in a Medical Assistant role, asks the patient a series of questions to complete the history). The system can use the model’s output to generate these follow-up questions dynamically. Another limitation is **bias** in training data – the models might perform worse or give biased advice for underrepresented groups or uncommon conditions . Rigorous evaluation across diverse patient scenarios is needed. Also, even though both models have been tuned for safety, they might occasionally produce **content that is sensitive or disallowed** (e.g. a frank discussion of prognosis that could distress a patient, or mentioning another patient’s info by accident if the context handling fails). Thus, an **output filtering layer** is recommended: before any AI response reaches an end-user, the system should scan it for forbidden content, privacy leaks, or tone issues. OpenAI provides a moderation API and Anthropic uses constitutional AI checks – these can be leveraged as automated filters. Finally, from a **legal/compliance** perspective, any direct clinical recommendations given by an AI could raise liability questions; institutions deploying such systems often require patients to consent and acknowledge the AI’s limitations, and clinicians to ultimately take responsibility for decisions. Transparency is key – users (whether patient or provider) should know when they’re interacting with an AI and what its role is.

## **Handling Clinical Language, Uncertainty, and Safety Alignment**

**Clinical Language Proficiency:** Both GPT-4 Turbo and Claude are highly proficient in medical terminology and jargon. They have been trained on large swaths of medical text (e.g. scientific articles, medical websites, possibly clinical notes) which gives them a natural fluency in *“doctor-speak.”* For instance, they understand abbreviations like “HTN” (hypertension) or “HPI” (history of present illness) and can generate detailed medical documentation. In fact, pilots integrating GPT-4 into clinical documentation workflows found it adept at drafting consultation notes and ICD-10 coding suggestions . Claude, in turn, has been integrated with medical coding and literature lookup tools  , indicating it too can navigate formal medical language. Importantly, these models can also **translate medical language to lay language**, which is essential in a patient-facing digital twin. The system’s *Translation Layer* can prompt the model to simplify complex terms for a 6th-grade reading level . For example, if a specialist agent responds, “The patient has idiopathic neuropathy likely secondary to long-standing diabetes,” the patient-facing agent can ask the model to rephrase: “They have nerve pain in their feet, and doctors aren’t sure of the cause, but it may be related to their diabetes.” This dual fluency – understanding clinicians and explaining to patients – is a strength of LLMs. In comparing GPT-4 vs Claude: both are good at this, though Claude is often noted for *verbose, highly detailed* explanations (which can be pared down if needed), whereas GPT-4 is concise unless asked for detail. The choice may come down to style preference or context window (Claude can take in an entire EHR note full of jargon and translate it in one go due to its large context). Overall, both models handle clinical language effectively, needing only minimal prompt guidance on the desired tone or complexity of output.

**Reasoning Under Uncertainty:** Medical scenarios are rife with uncertainty – incomplete information, ambiguous symptoms, or multiple possible diagnoses. A key aspect of a safe AI assistant is how it handles this uncertainty. GPT-4 and Claude both possess probabilistic reasoning abilities in their training; however, they differ slightly in expression. Anthropic’s Claude is explicitly designed with a **“constitutional AI”** approach that encourages it to be transparent about uncertainty and avoid overstating confidence . In practice, Claude often includes phrases like “it’s possible that…,” “another consideration is…,” and will list multiple possibilities rather than a single answer when appropriate. This can be advantageous for safety, as it reminds users that there isn’t always a definite answer. GPT-4, especially with careful prompt tuning or system messages, can also provide nuanced answers (“Given the information, X is most likely, but Y and Z are also in the differential.”). In earlier iterations, GPT-4 sometimes came across as confident even when unsure, but OpenAI’s health-tuned versions have likely added more hedging and explicit probability language to align with medical best practices. For example, in one emergency medicine study, GPT-4 tended to **recommend extra tests** and admissions – implicitly showing it leaned into “better safe than sorry” when unsure . This indicates the model’s reasoning will skew toward thoroughness under uncertainty. That can be a positive for patient safety (fewer missed diagnoses), but it requires calibration to avoid *analysis paralysis* or over-treatment. An ideal specialist agent might use an ensemble of these models: e.g. ask both GPT-4 and Claude for their reasoning and then reconcile or have a rule that if either flags high risk, the system treats it as high risk. In summary, both models handle uncertainty by listing possible explanations and suggesting confirmatory steps (tests, referrals). The **system integrator should encourage this behavior** via prompts (e.g. “If you are not fully confident, do not guess – explain what information would help reduce uncertainty.”). Moreover, capturing the model’s confidence in a structured way (like a likelihood score for each diagnosis) could be useful. GPT-4 can output JSON with probability estimates if prompted  , which the orchestrating agent can interpret to decide how to act (e.g. seek a second opinion agent if confidence is low).

**Safety Alignment Measures:** Both OpenAI and Anthropic have invested in aligning their models to health-related safety requirements. On OpenAI’s side, ChatGPT and GPT-4 have been fine-tuned with human feedback specifically to avoid harmful medical content. They typically refuse requests that are obviously dangerous (“How can I get prescription X without a doctor?” would be declined). ChatGPT Health adds *“purpose-built encryption and isolation”* for health data and will automatically prompt users to move a conversation into the protected Health space if it detects health-related content  . This is a form of **safety scaffolding** at the platform level – ensuring that any PHI or sensitive discussion gets special handling (not used for training, kept compartmentalized, etc.). Anthropic’s Claude, with its constitutional AI, has a predefined set of principles (like beneficence, non-maleficence, etc.) that guide its responses. In healthcare use, Anthropic explicitly notes Claude will include *contextual disclaimers and direct users to professionals for personalized help* . This means the model might automatically add “Please consult your doctor before making changes to your treatment.” at the end of advice – a useful safety feature to maintain. Both models can also be **instructed via system prompts** to follow specific clinical safety policies (e.g. “If the user asks for medical advice involving medication or invasive procedures, always remind them to check with a physician.”). In the multi-agent system, one can implement a *“safety agent”* or filter as part of the pipeline. For instance, after a specialist agent formulates an answer, another process (possibly another LLM or a set of rules) can check that answer for compliance with safety guidelines: no obviously harmful instructions, appropriate disclaimer included, no sensitive data leaked. This layered approach – model alignment + post-hoc filtering – is recommended to catch any lapse.

From a **privacy standpoint**, alignment also means ensuring **HIPAA compliance**. In the U.S. context, these models should be used in a way that protected health information is safeguarded. Both OpenAI and Anthropic now offer *HIPAA-ready enterprise solutions* (OpenAI’s “for Healthcare” suite and Anthropic’s Claude for Healthcare) where they sign business associate agreements and ensure data is not used to train base models  . However, in a multi-agent setting, an additional precaution is to **de-identify data before sending to the model**, unless using a fully HIPAA-compliant instance. The “Patient Digital Twin” concept explicitly aims to *“preserve privacy by sharing context, not raw PHI”* with specialist agents . For example, the patient’s name, exact birth date, address, etc., might be stripped out or replaced with placeholders. Only clinically relevant facts (age, gender, medical history, symptoms) are shared. This reduces the risk of accidental leaks and also means that even if the AI response were somehow seen by an unauthorized party, it wouldn’t immediately identify the patient. Privacy alignment also involves **audit logging**: every consultation between agents can be recorded (with de-identified data) in an audit log . This not only helps with debugging and improving the AI (traceability of why a recommendation was made) but is also important for compliance – if an AI ever gave a problematic suggestion, you have a record of it. It’s worth noting that introducing these AI agents could trigger organizational policies around medical advice and documentation. Many healthcare providers will treat the AI’s notes or recommendations as *draft* documentation or decision support, which then becomes part of the record once a human signs off. Thus, to align with both safety and privacy norms, the integration should include clear labeling of AI-generated content (to avoid confusion with clinician-authored notes) and encryption of any logs or communications between agents containing health data.

## **Integration Architecture and Agent Communication**

Integrating GPT-4 and Claude as **specialist agents** in a multi-agent digital twin system requires a thoughtful architecture. The core idea is an **agent-to-agent consultation mechanism** where a primary patient agent orchestrates and delegates tasks to various specialist sub-agents  . Below are key considerations and options for such an integration:

* **Orchestration via a Patient Agent:** In a digital twin system, the *“Patient Digital Twin”* or primary **Medical Assistant agent** serves as the central brain. This agent holds the comprehensive patient context (medical history, current data, conversation state) and has access to the data layer (EHR records, knowledge base). It interacts with the user (patient or clinician) and determines when a specialist consultation is needed  . For example, if a patient asks a complex question about a cardiac issue, the primary agent might invoke a “Cardiology Specialist” agent. The primary agent can be implemented with an LLM as well (possibly a smaller, faster model for basic Q&A and data retrieval, reserving GPT-4/Claude calls for heavy reasoning tasks). This orchestration agent uses **tools** or API calls to query specialist agents, ensuring each agent gets just the relevant subset of patient data needed for its task. This design was outlined in the project’s architecture with a *Consultation tool* that the Medical Assistant agent can call .
* **Agent-to-Agent Communication (MCP Protocol):** The system should define a clear protocol for agents to talk to each other. In the provided design, an **MCP (Model Context Protocol)** was created to standardize these interactions . Under MCP, when the primary agent consults a specialist, it sends a structured request containing the *de-identified context* and a specific question. For instance, it may send a JSON like: **{"specialty": "cardiology", "patient_case": {...}, "query": "Given this case, what is the recommended next step?"}**. The specialist agent (backed by GPT-4 or Claude) receives this and returns a result payload. Implementing MCP could be done via lightweight web services – each specialist agent could be an endpoint that the primary agent calls (using HTTP with an auth token, etc.) . The planning notes mention using libraries like **httpx** for async HTTP calls between agents . This essentially creates a microservice architecture: e.g., a “CardioConsult API” that internally calls the LLM with a cardiology-specific prompt template, or a “LabInterpreter API” that calls an LLM with lab data parsing instructions. The MCP can also handle **session management** if needed (though ideally each specialist call is stateless – the patient agent includes all necessary context in the request so the specialist doesn’t retain data between calls ). The protocol might include metadata like request IDs, timestamps, and an **audit log ID** to track the consultation . A robust MCP will ensure that if a specialist agent fails or returns an error (e.g., if the LLM call times out), the primary agent can handle it gracefully (perhaps retry or report an error) . In summary, a well-defined agent communication schema allows plug-and-play integration of different model providers – e.g. you could have an OpenAI GPT-4-powered agent for one specialty and a Claude-powered agent for another, each just following the MCP interface contracts.
* **Specialist Agent Design:** Each specialist agent can be thought of as a **wrapper around an LLM** with domain-specific prompting. One approach is to instantiate a GPT-4 or Claude model with a crafted system prompt that defines its persona and output format. For example: *“You are a board-certified cardiologist AI consulting on a case. You will receive patient data and must provide a detailed recommendation. Use medical terminology appropriate for physicians.”* The prompt can also include instructions about format (structured vs narrative) and policies (e.g. “Do not output any patient-identifying info; focus only on relevant facts”). When the primary agent calls this specialist, it inserts the patient’s de-identified data and question into the prompt and sends it to the model via the respective API (OpenAI or Anthropic). The response comes back to the primary agent for processing. An alternative approach is to fine-tune specialized models for each domain (if data and resources allow), but given GPT-4 and Claude’s broad knowledge, prompt specialization is usually sufficient. The system could also maintain *multiple specialized prompts for one model* – for instance, use GPT-4 with a radiology prompt when calling the “Radiology agent,” and GPT-4 with a different prompt for the “Pharmacist agent.” This avoids needing separate models per specialty, instead leveraging one model’s versatility. In a multi-agent digital twin, common specialist roles might include: **Primary Care (general diagnostics)**, **Pharmacy** (medication reconciliation and interaction checking), **Lab Analyst**, **Condition Specialists** (cardiology, neurology, etc. for complex cases), and **Health Coach** (for lifestyle and adherence counseling). By modularizing these, the system can scale – adding a new specialist agent is as simple as defining its prompt and endpoint, provided the core LLM has the knowledge (or access to knowledge via tools).
* **Response Formats: FHIR, JSON, or Natural Language:** A crucial integration decision is how agents format their outputs to each other and to end-users. Since this digital twin system interfaces both with humans (patients, providers) *and* with software (EHR databases, audit logs), **structured output** can be extremely useful. Both GPT-4 Turbo and Claude support structured formats in their responses when instructed. OpenAI’s models have a function calling and JSON mode where the model will output a JSON object following a given schema. Anthropic’s Claude, while oriented to plain text, can also be prompted to output valid JSON or even FHIR resources (especially with their new FHIR agent skill) . For inter-agent communication, using JSON or FHIR **ensures unambiguous data transfer**. For example, a differential diagnosis agent might return:

```
{
  "differential": [
    {"condition": "Pulmonary embolism", "probability": "high", "rationale": "Risk factors present and acute symptoms"},
    {"condition": "Pneumonia", "probability": "medium", "rationale": "Fever and cough suggest this, but no consolidation on X-ray"}
  ],
  "recommended_tests": ["D-dimer", "CT pulmonary angiogram"],
  "warning": "This is not a definitive diagnosis. Recommend clinical correlation."
}
```

* Such a response can be parsed by the primary agent or recorded directly. If using FHIR, one could map results to FHIR **DiagnosticReport** or **ClinicalImpression** resources, which would make integration with hospital EHRs easier (the system could automatically post a ClinicalImpression resource representing the AI’s assessment, tagged as AI-generated). The advantage of structured output is also for **auditability** and potentially regulatory compliance – it’s easier to review a standardized object than free text. On the other hand, **natural language** output is necessary when communicating directly with users or when the nuance of explanation is needed. In practice, the system can do both: internal agent-to-agent replies could be structured, then the primary agent (or a dedicated Translation agent) can convert that into a user-facing narrative. For instance, the differential JSON above might be turned into: *“The AI cardiologist considered a blood clot in the lungs to be very likely given your symptoms. It also thought about pneumonia. It suggests tests like a D-dimer or a CT scan. ****Please note:**** this isn’t a final diagnosis; you should see a doctor for confirmation.”* The architecture can support multiple **formatting options** – perhaps allowing the request to specify the desired format. Indeed, the MCP protocol could include a field like **"response_format": "FHIR"** or **"response_format": "plain_text"**. Anthropic’s connectors and OpenAI’s function calls essentially do this kind of formatting negotiation  .
* **Integration with Data Layers:** The digital twin system will sit on top of various data sources (the EHR database, knowledge bases, etc.). The LLM agents may need to query those sources to be accurate. There are two patterns to achieve this:

  1. **Pre-fetch and Embed:** The primary agent (or a separate retrieval agent) can fetch relevant data from the structured databases *before* calling the LLM, and include that data in the prompt. For example, if the user asks “Why is my blood pressure still high?”, the system could retrieve the last few blood pressure readings, medication lists, and any recent physician notes from the database. It then gives those as context to GPT-4/Claude. The models can interpret data on the fly (e.g. recognizing a trend of increasing BP values or identifying that the patient isn’t on a certain drug) and answer accordingly. This is facilitated by the large context windows available (Claude’s expansive context is especially helpful for feeding whole documents or long histories).
  2. **Tool Use by LLM:** Newer LLM integration frameworks allow the model to invoke tools (APIs) mid-conversation. For instance, GPT-4 could be given the ability to call a **get_lab_result(patient_id, test_name)** function if it decides it needs the latest lab value to answer a question. OpenAI’s function calling and Anthropic’s planned tool use can enable this approach  . In a multi-agent design, one could actually implement tool use by having the model output a structured request that the orchestrator picks up and executes. E.g. the specialist agent might say (in JSON), **"action": "query_db", "parameters": {"lab": "HbA1c"}** if during its reasoning it needs that info; the primary agent intercepts this, performs the query, and then resumes the consultation with the result. This is more complex but can make the AI more accurate and context-aware dynamically.

  Regardless of approach, integration must ensure that only **authorized and minimal data** is shared with the LLMs (principle of least privilege). For HIPAA compliance, if using a third-party API (OpenAI/Anthropic cloud), one would either use their HIPAA-compliant offering or de-identify. In some cases, organizations might deploy models on-premise for sensitive data, but GPT-4 and Claude are not generally available for on-prem deployment as of 2026, so the likely path is using their cloud with proper agreements.
* **Placement in the System Stack:** We can envision the multi-agent digital twin system having distinct layers where these models operate:

  * **Frontend (Patient-Facing) Layer:** This is the chat interface or application that the patient interacts with (for instance, a mobile app or web portal chat). The agent serving this layer (which could be the primary patient agent or a dedicated health coach agent) should prioritize **simplicity, clarity, and safety**. It might run on a moderated version of GPT-4/Claude or even a distilled model for cost-efficiency, and primarily handle conversational flow and educational responses. For complex questions, it forwards the query to backend specialist agents and then translates the answer back into layperson terms  . This patient-facing agent also enforces role-based access: in “patient mode” it will not show certain sensitive data or will simplify details, whereas if a provider is conversing, it could reveal more technical data  .
  * **Backend (Consultation) Layer:** This is where GPT-4, Claude, and potentially other AI models act as **consultants**. It’s essentially the brain of the digital twin performing clinical reasoning and data analysis out of the user’s direct view. Here we use the full power of the models (with longer outputs and technical detail) since the results will either be consumed by providers or distilled for patients. For example, a “Primary Care AI” agent in this layer might take the patient’s entire profile as context and generate an assessment and plan, much like a physician writes in a note. That output might be used as a draft for a clinical note or a summary for the patient. Because this layer is not directly user-facing, it can also output more **frank or raw evaluations** (flagging “possible malignancy – needs rule out” which a doctor would understand the uncertainty of, but a patient might panic if they saw directly). The primary patient agent mediates what portion of the backend output is shared and how.
  * **Integration/Augmentation Layer:** This layer connects AI outputs with existing healthcare systems and workflows. For instance, if the backend AI produces a recommendation to “schedule an echo in 3 months,” the system could interface with scheduling systems (perhaps via an API) to facilitate that – or at least prompt a human to schedule it. If the AI summarizes a visit, that summary could be inserted into the EHR as a draft note for the clinician to review. In U.S. healthcare, aligning with HL7 FHIR standards is crucial . The system might convert AI outputs to FHIR resources to document them properly or to trigger certain clinical decision support hooks in the EHR. For example, an AI-identified drug interaction could be translated into a FHIR **DetectedIssue** resource that the EHR can display as an alert. This integration layer is also where **privacy controls and audit logs** live – ensuring all AI interactions are recorded securely and that any external model calls are abstracted behind a service that can redact or log PHI as needed.

Different architectures can distribute these layers differently. One **architecture option** is to have a single monolithic AI service handling everything (patient chat and specialist reasoning all in one model instance) – simpler, but harder to maintain privacy and specialization. Another option (the one we’ve been discussing) is a **modular multi-agent architecture**: multiple specialized services orchestrated by a central agent. This latter option aligns well with the *digital twin* vision because it mirrors how a human care team works (with GPs, specialists, nurses – each with a role, coordinating on a case). It also offers flexibility: you could swap out the underlying model for a specialist agent if a better one comes along (for example, perhaps use a future Google Med-PaLM for certain tasks, or a smaller local model for cost on trivial queries, etc., all behind the MCP interface).

## **Auditing, Safety Filters, and Ongoing Compliance**

To maintain **safety and trust**, the outputs of these AI agents must be carefully managed through auditing and filtering:

* **Audit Trails:** Every agent-to-agent consultation and AI-generated suggestion should be logged in a secure audit trail . The log entry can include the question posed, the de-identified patient context provided, and the AI agent’s response. This is invaluable for later review – whether for debugging a mistake, providing transparency in case of an adverse event, or even for regulatory reporting if required. In a HIPAA context, audit logs also help track any information disclosures. The design calls for a **ConsultationAuditLog** to record specialist consultations . These logs should be treated as sensitive (since they may contain medical info without direct identifiers). Access to them must be restricted to authorized personnel. From a development standpoint, having logs of AI reasoning also allows **continuous improvement**: e.g. a clinical team could periodically review a sample of AI consults to ensure quality and adjust prompts/rules accordingly. If an AI agent gave a poor recommendation, it can be traced and that scenario used to retrain or refine the system.
* **Real-time Filtering:** Before an AI-generated message reaches an end-user (patient or provider), it should pass through a filter. OpenAI’s moderation system, for example, can check outputs for hate, self-harm encouragement, sexual or medical misinformation, etc., and flag or remove problematic content. In a healthcare context, we’d especially filter for **medical disinformation** and privacy leaks. For instance, if somehow an agent’s response included a person’s name or contact info (which should not happen if de-identified, but imagine a scenario where the patient themself input a relative’s name in their question – the AI might mention it in the answer), the system could automatically mask or drop that. Similarly, if a model tried to recommend something obviously dangerous (“Take 10 pills of…”) the filter could catch that and either remove it or trigger a human review. Anthropic’s Claude tends to have fewer overt toxic outputs due to its training, but caution is still warranted for subtle issues (e.g. unintentional bias or insensitive phrasing about a health condition). Therefore, implementing a combination of **automated filters and perhaps a final rule-based scan** is advisable. The final scan could be simple checks like: Ensure any medical advice output includes an appropriate caution/disclaimer; ensure no prohibited phrases are present; ensure the reading level is appropriate for patient communications (maybe using a readability algorithm).
* **Scaffolding Responses for Safety:** Another technique is to **scaffold the AI’s reasoning or responses through templates** that enforce safety. For example, one could use a response template for patient-facing answers: “1. **Answer the question in simple terms.** 2. **Give a suggestion for next steps or healthy habits.** 3. **Include a caution to consult a professional.**”. The AI would then fill in these sections. This ensures that every answer, no matter which model, has a built-in safety net of advising professional help when needed. For provider-facing outputs, scaffolding might include sections like “Potential diagnosis (with confidence %): …; Supporting evidence: …; Advice: …; References: …”. By structuring the output, we reduce the chance that the model goes off on tangents or omits critical context. Both GPT-4 and Claude can follow such multi-part instructions quite well due to their training on following human instructions.
* **Human Oversight and Review:** Especially in early deployment, having a human (clinician) review a sample of AI outputs is an important safety measure. This might be in the form of a “shadow mode” deployment – the AI gives recommendations but the care team doesn’t act on them until they’ve been verified in parallel trials. Over time, as confidence grows, certain low-risk tasks can be fully automated (for instance, *administrative responses like appointment reminders or insurance form assistance* could be done by AI with minimal review, since the harm potential is low). In contrast, high-risk outputs (like a diagnostic suggestion in an ambiguous case) might always require human sign-off. The system architecture should allow configuring these workflows. For example, an AI draft note might go into a physician’s approval queue in the EHR; an AI’s message to a patient might need a nurse to click “approve” before it’s sent if it contains medical advice beyond basic education.
* **Compliance Checks:** Since we prioritize U.S. context, compliance with HIPAA and other regulations (like FDA’s emerging AI guidelines) is crucial. The integration architecture should thus ensure that PHI is never sent to external services without a BAA and encryption. In practice, using OpenAI’s and Anthropic’s *enterprise HIPAA-compliant endpoints* is advisable for production – these ensure data is encrypted in transit and at rest, and not used for training models  . Additionally, if the AI system offers any sort of diagnosis or treatment assessment to providers, one must be aware of FDA’s definition of Clinical Decision Support (CDS) that falls under regulation . To stay on the safe side, the system might be deployed initially as an “informational tool” rather than an autonomous CDS. Keeping a human in the loop and clearly labeling AI outputs as suggestions (not absolute conclusions) helps in meeting the FDA’s criteria for tools that do **not** require premarket approval (the draft guidance indicates tools that only *assist* and where a human can independently review the basis of the suggestion might avoid being classified as a medical device ). Nonetheless, monitoring the regulatory landscape is important – the architecture might need to log certain data for future auditing if the AI becomes part of clinical decision-making.

In conclusion, integrating OpenAI’s GPT-4 Turbo and Anthropic’s Claude into a multi-agent digital twin healthcare system is a promising approach to augment healthcare delivery. These models exhibit **expert-level capabilities** in many specialist tasks – from diagnosing to interpreting data – and, with proper safeguards, can function as valuable collaborators in both patient-facing and provider-facing roles. The recommended strategy is to use them as **“plug-in” specialist agents** behind a primary patient agent, communicating via a structured protocol (such as the MCP) and exchanging information in controlled formats like JSON/FHIR. By doing so, we can compartmentalize their knowledge, preserve patient privacy, and capture the unique strengths of each model (GPT-4’s reasoning precision and Claude’s contextual breadth) within one system. Careful attention to **safety, privacy, and compliance** – through alignment tuning, filters, audit logs, and human oversight – will ensure these AI agents remain reliable and trustworthy. Ultimately, in a U.S. HIPAA-regulated context, this architecture can empower patients to engage with their digital health data more deeply and help clinicians deliver more informed, efficient care, all while keeping the “human in the loop” for final judgment calls.
